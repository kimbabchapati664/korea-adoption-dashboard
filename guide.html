  1. 1

    Gather everything you already have

    Before you contact anyone, put every paper, photo and letter from your adoption in one place and read it once, slowly. Your adoptive family may have more than you think.

    • Ask your adoptive family for the full folder: agency letters, the "social history" report, medical forms, your Korean passport or travel document, arrival papers, the adoption decree, and any photos.
    • Write down anything that looks like a number, a Korean name, a date or a place. Copy it exactly, including dashes and letters.
    • Run it all through the Roots Finder to see what each item means and where it leads.

  3. 3

    File your Adoption Information Disclosure Request

    입양정보공개청구. This is the door everything else opens from. It is free, it is online, and you have to file it yourself.

    • You get without anyone's permission: background information such as your adoption date, the stated reason for relinquishment, your pre-adoption name, your date and time of birth, and your birth parents' ages at the time.
    • Needs your birth parents' consent: their identifying information (name, date of birth, address, contact). If consent is not given, G.O.A.'L advises waiting a year and asking again.
    • Under 18? You need your adoptive parents' consent. Adopted under the Civil Act rather than the special adoption laws? This route is not open to you; contact NCRC or G.O.A.'L about alternatives.
    • Include your agency case number exactly as printed. It helps NCRC find your folder.

  4. 4

    Register your DNA, in two places

    A match only happens if you and your relative are in the same database. Use the free Korean police database, then widen the net.

    • Korean police DNA database (free). Once your disclosure request is filed and NCRC confirms there is no usable birth-parent information, NCRC issues a "Certificate of Adoptee Without Identifying Birth Parent Information for Genetic Testing." You then give a sample at a Korean embassy or consulate (35 missions across 14 countries), which sends it to the Korean National Police Agency. Searching birth families register in the same database.
    • To start: email your signed application, consent form and a copy of your ID to familysearch@ncrc.or.kr, then contact your nearest Korean embassy or consulate. NCRC family-search line: +82-2-6454-8500, weekdays 09:00–16:00 KST.
    • 325KAMRA (adoptee-founded). Mails free FamilyTreeDNA kits worldwide and collects DNA from birth families inside Korea. There is a non-refundable $10 admin fee plus return postage.
    • Already tested elsewhere? Download your raw data and upload it to FamilyTreeDNA, GEDmatch and MyHeritage, which accept uploads.

  5. 5

    Make yourself findable

    Your birth family may be looking too. Put yourself where they would look.

    • Post on NCRC's free public search boards. Both adoptees and Korean families post; entries show pre-adoption name, sex, date of birth, country adopted to and place of birth.
    • Register with the Hope Registry, a matching registry where adoptees and Korean birth families both sign up.
    • Write your Korean name in Hangul and your date of birth in Korean format. The Finder generates these for you.

  6. 6

    Get someone on the ground

    The search is easier with people who have done it before, and with people who will understand you at 2am.

    • G.O.A.'L (Seoul, adoptee-led since 1998): 1:1 search consultations, file review, search itineraries in Korea, interpretation before and after reunion, F-4 visa and dual-citizenship guidance. Most services need membership. +82-2-325-6585 · gcr@goal.or.kr
    • KoRoot (Jongno, Seoul): guesthouse for returning adoptees, family tracing, legal support and counselling.
    • Adoptee Hub: birth search, reunion support, counselling, translation and group trips.
    • Find your country's adoptee association through the IKAA network.

  7. 7

    Plan a visit to Korea (when you are ready)

    Not required to start, but a file review in person often turns up details that do not survive a summary.

    • In-person file review at NCRC's Seoul office or the archive in Goyang. Reservations open 14 to 50 days ahead and slots are limited. Your disclosure request must already be on file.
    • Staying longer than 90 days? The F-4 visa is for people of Korean descent; NCRC issues the Adoption Verification Certificate the application needs.
    • Korean nationality: adoptees can restore Korean nationality and hold dual citizenship, also with the Adoption Verification Certificate.
    • KoRoot's guesthouse in Jongno is the usual gentle first stay.

  8. 8

    If a match or a reply comes

    This is the part people plan least and need most. Go slowly.

    • Identifying information is only released with your birth family's consent. A "not now" is not always a "never."
    • Ask for an interpreter who has done reunions before. G.O.A.'L interprets before and after reunion; 325KAMRA helps with first contact and translation after a DNA match.
    • Line up support for yourself first: an adoptee group, a counsellor, or a friend who will be on the phone afterwards.
    • In crisis while in Korea: NCRC +82-2-6454-8645 / 8646, or G.O.A.'L +82-2-325-6585.
    Take your time
Two things to know about NCRC. It is the only holder of your file, and its services above are free. NCRC also suffered a data breach between 30 April and 2 May 2026 that exposed sensitive adoptee records; affected people were notified on 3 May 2026. You deserve to know that when you decide what to share.
